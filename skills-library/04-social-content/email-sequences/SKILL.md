# Email Sequences — Lifecycle Email Flow Design

For automated, trigger-based email flows — distinct from one-off newsletter or campaign sends.

## Design by trigger, not by content first

Every sequence starts from a specific user action or state, not a content idea:
- **Welcome/onboarding** — triggered by signup; goal is first-value realization, not immediate upsell.
- **Abandoned cart/incomplete action** — triggered by a stalled flow; goal is removing the specific friction that caused the stall.
- **Post-purchase/post-activation** — triggered by completion; goal is retention and expansion, not just a receipt.
- **Re-engagement/win-back** — triggered by inactivity; goal is reactivation, with an honest acknowledgment of the gap, not pretending nothing happened.

## Sequence structure principles

1. **Each email has exactly one job.** An onboarding email trying to teach a feature AND upsell AND ask for a review does none of the three well.
2. **Frontload value in early emails** before any ask — the first 1-2 emails in a welcome sequence should deliver something useful with no ask attached, building trust before the CTA-heavy emails.
3. **Space emails to match realistic user behavior**, not an arbitrary daily cadence — a B2B trial user might need day 0/2/5/10 spacing; a consumer app abandoned-cart flow might need hours/1-day/3-day spacing.
4. **Every email needs a single clear CTA** — competing CTAs in one email split conversion across both and convert on neither well.

## Exit conditions matter as much as entry triggers

Define explicitly when a user should be pulled out of a sequence (they converted, they unsubscribed, they took the target action) — sequences that keep sending after the goal is already achieved read as tone-deaf and hurt trust.

## Measurement

Track sequence-level goal completion (did the welcome sequence lead to activation), not just per-email open/click rates in isolation — a high open rate on an email that doesn't move the user toward the sequence's actual goal isn't success.
