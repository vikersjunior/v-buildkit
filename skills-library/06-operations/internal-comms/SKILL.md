# Internal Comms — Status Reports & Leadership Updates

For internal-facing written communication — distinct from external marketing copy in register and structure.

## Status/project update structure

1. **Bottom-line-up-front** — the single most important fact (on track / at risk / blocked, and why) in the first line. Busy readers should get the answer before deciding whether to read further, not have to read the whole update to find the status.
2. **What happened since last update** — concrete, specific progress, not "made progress on X."
3. **What's next** — the specific next steps and expected timing.
4. **Risks/blockers** — stated explicitly and specifically, with what's needed to unblock (and from whom) — a status update that hides a real risk to look better in the moment costs more credibility later when the risk materializes anyway.

## Leadership update structure

Similar bottom-line-up-front discipline, but zoom out one level: frame in terms of the business outcome/goal this connects to, not just the team's activity — leadership needs to know why this matters at their altitude, not the full operational detail.

## Company-wide announcement/FAQ structure

- Lead with what's changing and why, in plain language, before any procedural detail.
- Anticipate the actual questions people will have (not the questions convenient to answer) and answer them directly in an FAQ section, including uncomfortable ones if they're genuinely likely to be asked — dodging an obvious question in the doc just means it gets asked anyway, less controlled, in a different channel.

## Tone discipline across all internal comms

Direct and specific beats diplomatic and vague — internal audiences generally have more context and less patience for hedged language than external audiences do. Avoid corporate euphemism ("resourcing challenges" for "we're understaffed") — it reads as evasive to an internal audience who usually already knows the real situation.
